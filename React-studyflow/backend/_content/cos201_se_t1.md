# Software Crisis, NATO 1968, and the Birth of Software Engineering

## Why did software become difficult to build?

Early programs were small enough for one person to understand from beginning to end. As computers became more powerful, organizations asked software to control aircraft, keep financial records, and manage large systems. Teams and codebases grew, but the way software was planned and built often remained informal.

The result was called the **software crisis**: projects were late or over budget, unreliable after delivery, difficult to maintain, or failed to meet what users actually needed. The core problem was not simply that programmers could not write code. It was that building dependable, complex software required coordinated methods for requirements, design, testing, teams, and change.

## What happened at NATO in 1968?

At a 1968 NATO conference, researchers and practitioners discussed these problems and helped establish the term **software engineering**. The word “engineering” was deliberately provocative: software should be developed systematically, using disciplined processes and evidence, rather than relying only on individual craftsmanship.

The conference did not instantly solve the crisis or create one universal method. It helped make the scale of the problem visible and encouraged a field focused on repeatable, organized development.

## From writing a program to delivering a system

A useful distinction is:

- **Programming** writes and changes code.
- **Software engineering** coordinates the full life of a software product: understanding a need, deciding what to build, designing it, implementing it, testing it, deploying it, and maintaining it.

Imagine a student registration system. Code is only one part. The team must understand registration rules, handle peak demand, protect student records, test unusual cases, train staff, and repair defects after launch. A program that runs on a developer's laptop but loses registrations at peak time is not a successful system.

## The engineering response

Software engineering applies organized methods to control complexity and risk. Teams use requirements to agree on the problem, architecture to divide it into manageable parts, version control to coordinate changes, reviews to catch mistakes, and tests to provide evidence that behavior matches expectations.

These practices do not guarantee perfection. They make decisions and failures easier to detect, explain, and correct. The right process depends on the system's size, risk, users, and rate of change.

## A modern definition and its implications

Software engineering is the systematic, disciplined, and measurable approach to developing, operating, maintaining, and evolving software. Each word matters:

- **Systematic:** work follows a considered process rather than improvisation alone.
- **Disciplined:** technical decisions and quality checks are applied consistently.
- **Measurable:** teams use evidence such as tests, defect rates, delivery progress, and user outcomes.
- **Evolving:** software continues to change after its first release.

**Check your understanding:** A group delivers an app on time, but users cannot complete the main task and the team cannot safely change it. Was delivery alone enough to establish success? No; user needs, quality, and maintainability are part of the engineering problem too.

## Keep the lesson in perspective

The software crisis is not a claim that every software project fails. It describes recurring difficulties that appear when complexity grows faster than a team's ability to plan, coordinate, verify, and maintain a system. Software engineering exists to manage those difficulties and improve the odds of delivering useful, dependable software.
